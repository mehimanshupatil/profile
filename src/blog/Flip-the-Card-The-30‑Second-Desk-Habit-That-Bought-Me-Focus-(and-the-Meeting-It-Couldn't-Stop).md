It was 9:10 on a rainy Monday in Bengaluru. My calendar had one long block: 90 minutes to ship a refactor that several other engineers were waiting on. I’d cleared my browser of tabs, brewed coffee, and then my colleague walked up and asked, casually, “Can you quickly look at this?” Thirty seconds later, another person leaned over. Two minutes after that, someone pinged asking for an impromptu design review.

By 9:20 my brain had become a string of half‑started thoughts. The 90‑minute block finished with three unfinished refactors and the sinking feeling that I'd spent the morning in other people's context. This was normal. Open-plan teams in Indian product companies—Bengaluru office, eight people to a row, managers who value "responsiveness"—mean interruptions are part of the job. But I wanted something small I could do in 30 seconds that actually changed behaviour.

So I made a card.

What the card is and how I use it

I bought a pack of index cards for ₹60, wrote DND on one side and "Back in 25" on the other, laminated it at a nearby shop for ₹40, and stuck a tiny magnet on the back. The whole thing cost less than a sandwich.

The rule is simple:

- Flip the card to DND and put it on your laptop (or prop it up) when you start focused work.
- Set a 25‑minute timer (Pomodoro style, but with a difference).
- Ignore non‑urgent pings except those tagged with the team's escalation emoji (we use :bangbang: on Slack).
- After 25 minutes, flip the card to "Back in 25", stand up, stretch, and triage messages for five minutes. Then repeat if needed.

Why a physical card, not just Slack status or a calendar block

I tried digital-first: set statuses, calendar blocks, even added “DO NOT DISTURB” to my meeting title. Those helped a bit, but they didn’t change people’s in-person behaviour. Colleagues still walked over. Managers still dropped by. A Slack status is invisible to someone standing next to you.

The card is visible in real life. You can see it across the desk. It forces the person approaching to pause for half a beat—enough to ask “Is it urgent?” and either decide to wait or use the escalation channel. That half-second pause is the whole thing. It made people think twice before interrupting me for tiny things.

The 25‑minute window matters. It’s short enough that people don’t feel dismissed and long enough that I can get into flow. The visible ritual—flip, set timer, close tabs—signals something different is happening than a calendar block.

How I stopped being "the person who was always available"

There were social tradeoffs. At first, people assumed I was becoming rude. A few teammates interpreted the card as gatekeeping knowledge: “Why won’t you just help me for two minutes?” So I made a small change: if someone looked stuck, they could place the same card on my end of the desk and I would come over. That swap culture made the card reciprocal, which removed the perceived unhelpful vibe.

I also added a visible emergency channel. We agreed that anything tagged with :bangbang: (we kept it because it's obvious) required immediate attention. That gave colleagues a clear path and reduced the "is it important?" guessing.

The week it failed — and what I learned

Two months in, the card worked so well I started using it at home for WFH mornings. Then it failed spectacularly.

It was a Thursday evening. I had the card flipped, headphones on, and was deep in a migration. A paging system (we had a small on‑call rota) sent an alert at 6:11pm, and for reasons I still don’t fully understand, the alert didn’t tag the :bangbang: channel. The on‑call engineer assumed I was unavailable because of the card and went to sleep after blasting an 'FYI' in a general channel. The issue escalated to a partial outage. We lost paying customers for about 45 minutes. The post‑mortem called out "unread escalations" and my DND card.

I felt awful. I’d taken a tool that increased my focus and used it in a context where strict availability mattered. The failure wasn't the card itself; it was my failure to partition contexts. I had mixed deep work hours with on‑call responsibility without adequately informing the rota and the process.

After that we codified two rules:

- Never use the card while on active on‑call shifts.
- Always route alerts through the explicit :bangbang: channel or directly to phone numbers that bypass DND rituals.

The honest tradeoff

The honest constraint here is cultural: Indian office norms often reward “always being available,” and a small physical token won't erase that. The card reduces casual interruptions, but it also introduces a social signal you must manage. People might feel shut out until you make the rule reciprocal and explain escalation paths.

Also, it doesn’t scale to emergency contexts—pager systems, billing alerts—unless you pair it with an operational protocol. The card is not a replacement for on‑call discipline.

What actually changed for me

Three things changed in practice:

- I reclaimed two reliable 90‑minute mornings a week for focused work. That is, real uninterrupted time.
- My team learned a tiny coordination protocol—swap the card, use :bangbang:—and it stuck because it was physical and small.
- I stopped confusing "I am focused" with "I am unavailable for emergencies." Context matters; the card made me intentional about it.

One takeaway

If you're drowning in interruptions at work, buy a cheap card, make a 25‑minute rule, and be explicit about escalation. The card's purpose is not to be rude; it's to force a pause that protects flow and forces the team to choose: is this worth interrupting me for, or can it wait 25 minutes? That second question is where most of your time gets reclaimed.

Flip the card. Give yourself a short window. And for God's sake, if you're on call, put the card away.
